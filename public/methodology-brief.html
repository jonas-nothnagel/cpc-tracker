Nature-Climate Policy Coherence Analyzer · Methodology

How the analysis works

Turning national policy data into traceable, evidence-based insights on policy coherence: where commitments reinforce one another, where they may compete, and where finance and implementation gaps remain.

Purpose

Across emerging National Development Plans, Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), National Biodiversity Strategies and Action Plans (NBSAPs), National Adaptation Plans (NAPs) and Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN) targets, ambition has risen for the planet and its people. However, these national commitments are commonly created, implemented and monitored in siloes, with a lack of clarity on how they speak to each other. The analysis provides data-based evidence on the synergies, opportunities and gaps based on the policy, finance, and implementation information provided. It is a decision-support system, not a decision-maker: it surfaces potential patterns for national experts to review, but the final interpretation rests with policymakers.

What the analysis covers

The same analytical method is applied at three levels, as far as each country’s data allows.

The method

Every result is produced by the same transparent sequence, organised in three phases. The analysis reads only the policy text it is given and never introduces policy content of its own.

Phase 1 · Extraction
Documents to targets
Each page is read in full, boilerplate is set aside, and every target is extracted word for word with its source. Duplicates are merged, and each figure, year and named framework is verified against the original document.
→
Phase 2 · Analysis
Targets to coherence
Each target is flagged for whether it is measurable and time-bound, and classified against selected reference frameworks. Every cross-document pair is then assessed: one specialist agent decomposes each target into its parts, and a second judges how the two align.
→
Phase 3 · Synthesis
Pairs to patterns
The individual judgements are rolled into coherency scores and coverage, recurring potential misalignment is surfaced, and the same method is extended to finance and implementation where the data exists.

Each pair is placed on a five-state scale. Four positive levels describe how strongly two targets align; a single review state, Potential misalignment, flags a pair for expert examination rather than declaring a conflict.

Inside the analysis

Classification against selected themes

Targets are scored against published reference frameworks: IPCC greenhouse gas inventory sectors, the Global Biodiversity Expenditure (GLOBE) taxonomy, and the seven climate-resilience targets of the UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience (the Global Goal on Adaptation). Categories trace to their sources and are configurable per country. Each target keeps one primary category plus any others it clearly relates to, so the same evidence can be read through different lenses without re-analysis.

Comparison in two steps

The comparison is deliberately separated into two stages. A target is first decomposed into its goal, action, area, actors and expected outcome; alignment is then assessed from that structure. This keeps judgements consistent across countries and policy frameworks, and grounded in policy substance rather than shared keywords.

Why “potential misalignment”, not “conflict”

The negative side of the scale is deliberately cautious. A potential misalignment is a prompt for review, never a verdict: each one records what kind it is (competing goals, a shared resource, or delivery pressure), how manageable it appears, and how confident the reading is, so a reviewer sees why a pair was flagged.

Synthesis and framing

The analysis produces short factual insights and visuals, not extended AI-written narratives. Where next steps are suggested, they are framed as possibilities to explore (“could”, “may”), never as instructions.

Worked example · Mongolia

The NBSAP target to include all territory in spatial planning is paired with the NDC target to secure water availability through new infrastructure. Both concern land and water governance, but the infrastructure could press on the ecosystems the spatial-planning target protects. The analysis records the pair as a Potential misalignment of the delivery type, assessed as manageable and at medium confidence, and notes that coordination through spatial planning, zoning and safeguards would keep the two aligned. It is one of 46 pairs flagged for review among the 540 compared between the two documents, where overall coherence stands at 66%.

What the analysis produces

  • Visuals and short, factual insights on coverage, alignment patterns, and where coordination could be strengthened, rather than an automated report.
  • Switchable lenses, under the user’s control, so the same analysis can be regrouped by theme, by sector (GLOBE, IPCC, or a country’s own set), or by finance and implementation overlays, with no re-analysis.
  • A grounded assistant that answers questions using only the analysed evidence.

Safeguards

Human in the loop. Extracted targets are confirmed and edited before any analysis runs, and any AI rationale can be rated to improve future runs. Outputs are AI-assisted and require expert validation before use in policy processes.
Grounded and traceable. Only the provided data are classified and compared; every result links back to the source text it came from, and nothing is asserted that cannot be checked.
Data sovereignty. The analysis is model-agnostic: a country can choose which AI model the tool uses, keeping control of where its data is processed.
Frugal and Fair AI. A low-footprint design whose environmental cost is measured and reported openly.

The analysis is being built as an open-source Digital Public Good, so that national governments can eventually run it on their own data and infrastructure.
